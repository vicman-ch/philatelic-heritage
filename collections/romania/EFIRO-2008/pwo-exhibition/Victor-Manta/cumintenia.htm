A sad story of a work of art

Victor Manta, Switzerland

 

Constantin Brancuşi was a Romanian sculptor who trained initially as a carpenter and stonemason. He settled in Paris in 1904 where his early influences included African as well as oriental art. Although Rodin was another early influence, Brancuşi decided he wished to make much simpler work and began an evolutionary search for pure form. While never entirely rejecting the natural world, Brancusi undoubtedly succeeds in conveying a sense of gravity by reducing his work to a few basic elements. Paradoxically, this process also tends to highlight the complexity of thought that has gone into its making.
One of his early works, The Wisdom of the Earth (Cuminţenia pamântului, 1907) is a reflection of Brancusi's philosophy, that has its roots in a wisdom that is anterior to Christianity. The sculpture shows a woman that is very concentrated on herself, her figure releasing a mystery that transfigures her into an universal portrait. The statue expresses author's feeling of attachment toward the mother Earth but the source of inspiration of his work is actually unknown.

This statue, valuing now over 15 million dollars, was confiscated by the communist regime from its owner, the architect Gheorghe Romaşcu, who bought it from Brancuşi in 1910. The heirs of Romaşcu are fighting over through the legal system with the Romanian government for over 15 years.  The statue is in custody of the National Museum of Art.

The daughter of Gheorghe Romaşcu, Paula Ionescu, explained that the statue was in the possession of her father during 47 years. In the year 1957 the police took the statue "for an exhibition". It was never returned. Later the state officials have invented the story that the statue was bought from Mr. Romaşcu by the state but they never could present the smallest evidence to the justice.

Even if Paula Ionescu has documents that prove that she is one of the two legal owners of the work, she got so far (2006), in 15 years, only unfavorable sentences from the Romanian legal system. Now she put her hopes in the Supreme Tribunal,  the highest legal instance in Romania, and she is decided to appeal to the High Justice Court in The Haag if necessary.

At a certain moment the statue was mounted on a pedestal, under the supervisory of the state specialists. When this was done, the legs of the statue were broken, said Paula Ionescu. Before this accident the statue was evaluated at $ 30 millions but the accident reduced its value to a half. And even now the statue isn't assured by the National Museum of Art...On this page I show different views of the statue, in its original and then in the modified state, as well as the stamp "Cuminţenia pamântului", issued in 1967 (Scott #1917) and the miniature sheet that shows Brancuşi in his atelier, issued in 1982 (Scott #3070).

Eventually note that the face of the statue, on the stamp, looks somehow differently when compared with the original (was not intelligent or inspired enough for the "socialist realism"?).
