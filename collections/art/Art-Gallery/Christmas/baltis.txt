Miniature Sheet “Christmas decorations”
Christmassy signs of the time

People sending mail around the turn
of the year like to give their letters
and cards a festive look and this,
as we know, is best done with small,
decorative postage stamps which
enrich the exchange of personal
thoughts and often open the door
to the past, to the front parlours of
yesteryear.
In spring 2000, Swiss Post asked me if I
would like to tackle this topic. “Gladly!”
was my answer, because designing a
Christmas stamp set with genuine old
Christmas decorations was a special
challenge. The stamps were to radiate
charm and warmth and display signs and
symbols of days gone by. The plan was
for a stamp in the set to be issued each

November and for all of them to look like
a family in appearance and colouring.
How do you start on such a job? I found
the decorations at Anton P. Zobrist’s in
Ebmatingen, in the biggest collection
of old Christmas decorations in Europe.
Through talking with him and seeking
little gems, we found some beautiful
collectors’ pieces, light, delicate miniature
works of art of yesteryear, with differing
features and of differing origins and
design.
So the idea ripened of dedicating a
stamp to each geographical area,
balanced but still different from each
other. As the customer had not set
any specific design requirements, I was

able (with Mr. Zobrist’s advice) to select
some delightful small items from his
marvellous collection and take them to
my studio for the design period.
I made countless sketches as I progressed
towards finding the form and content
of these stamps. And it became clear as
I went on what was suitable and what
was not. The original drafts have to be
made in A4 size, as a crayon drawing,

with photos, as a collage or any combination
of the three. What is right on
a large scale, must also be right in the
7-fold reduction to postage-stamp size.
And that’s where much of the graphic
part of this work comes in: combining all
the elements of an idea in such a way
that picture, text, denomination etc.
harmonize and the message comes
across. Imagination alone is not enough –
the visual logic has to be right too.
With me, the process moves along in
three stages. The first is research, reading
up on the topic and talking to experts
in the field. The second is the design
(and often the blood, sweat and tears)
phase. The third is producing the final
artwork, that is the copy ready for
printing, where everything has to be
right, down to the finest detail.
My fascination with miniatures stems
from the fact that I can set cultural
values, enriching everyday life and
reproducing historical events in a form,
which is not only attractive but can also
often be disseminated a million times
over as a friendly letter-borne message.
